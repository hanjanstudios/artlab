Foreshortening & Dynamic Hand/Body Anatomy

Foreshortening Form Contour Negative Space

What It Is

Foreshortening compresses a form along the viewer's line of sight — a pointed arm or outstretched leg looks shorter and wider than its actual length because you're seeing it end-on.

Why Artists Use It

It's what separates flat, stiff figure drawing from figures that feel like they're reaching, punching, or lunging directly at the viewer.

How To Practice It — Step By Step

  1. 1
    Simplify to cylinders

    Break the limb or form into simple cylindrical sections rather than an outline.

  2. 2
    Find the nearest point

    Identify what part of the form is physically closest to the viewer — that part will be drawn largest.

  3. 3
    Compress, don’t shrink

    Overlap and stack the cylindrical sections toward the vanishing form rather than making them thinner.

  4. 4
    Check the negative space

    Compare the empty space around the limb to your reference — foreshortened forms often have unusual, exaggerated negative-space shapes that are easier to judge than the limb itself.

  5. 5
    Add contour lines

    Wrap a few lines around the form like rings on a tube to reinforce the sense of it coming toward you.

  6. 6
    Render form, not outline

    Use shading to show where the cylinder turns away from the viewer, not just a hard edge.

Try This

Photograph your own hand pointing straight at the camera and draw only the negative space around it first — it trains your eye to stop relying on “how long is this really.”
