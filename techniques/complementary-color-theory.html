Complementary & Saturated Color Theory

Complementary Colours Hue Saturation Vibration

What It Is

Complementary colors sit directly opposite each other on the color wheel (red/green, blue/orange, yellow/purple). Placed side by side at full saturation, they create maximum visual contrast and seem to “vibrate” against each other.

Why Artists Use It

It's how artists like Van Gogh and the Fauvists made color itself feel emotionally charged rather than just descriptive of local color.

How To Practice It — Step By Step

  1. 1
    Pick your dominant hue

    Choose the main color your piece will be built around.

  2. 2
    Find its complement

    Locate the color directly opposite it on a color wheel.

  3. 3
    Assign a ratio

    Use roughly 80% of your dominant hue and 20% of its complement, rather than a 50/50 split, so one color still reads as dominant.

  4. 4
    Place complements at transitions

    Use small, sharp touches of the complementary color exactly where two shapes meet, not spread evenly across the piece.

  5. 5
    Protect your focal point

    Avoid graying down your focal area by mixing the complements together there; save any muted mixing for background areas.

  6. 6
    Squint and check

    Step back and squint at your piece — the complementary passages should visually pop or vibrate against their surroundings.

Try This

Paint the same simple still life twice — once in any colors you like, once forcing yourself to use only one hue and its direct complement — and compare how differently they feel.
